The debt

Helen R. Hull

“Remorse” is a harsh word, a “gnawing-back upon the heart.” It is a harsh emotion, one that belongs primarily to middle age. Youth does not know it, except in brief, wild moments caused by trivial, quickly forgotten incidents. Regret that one has failed a friend, remorse that one has lied, or stolen, or quarreled with a brother. Temporary, because amends can be made, the quarrel patched up, the lie confessed. These brief moments might be a warning of a deeper, inconsolable grief; they are of the same nature, for all their brevity; but youth heeds no warning. In a way, this is essential to growth. For remorse is black and negative, it is gyves and fetters, ball and chain, it looks only backward, it impedes all progress, and too early knowledge of its dreadful gnawing would hold the youth incapable of motion. In youth nothing seems irrevocable, everything moves ahead, tomorrow and tomorrow are the days toward which one runs. And age, fortunately or it would know no peace, is all passion spent, even the passion of remorse.

But at some time in the middle years each man comes upon a regret that is inescapable, a remorse for which there is no alleviation. The friend whom he has failed is dead, his grave is heaped, the wreaths wither, amends are nothing but a crying in the night. The quarrel between the generations—the old quarrel, in which the youth must contend with the elders in order to be free—is ended with the mother’s death. There are left the trunks in which—oh, sentimentally, if you like—she had kept the baby shoes and little packets of baby hair, the diary in which she set down modestly her aspirations for you before your birth. You had meant someday to make her proud of you, to please her; it is too late now.

She has closed her eyes against the disappointing spectacle of your humdrum middle age; she has closed her lips upon her protests at your wilful, headlong mistakes; she has surrendered up her loneliness and her grief. There was a debt you should have paid, and now it is too late. Did you think you would escape payment, that there would be a moratorium on this debt, that time and death would outlaw it? You will learn that you will pay it in full, not to the past but to the future. An eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth, a grief for a grief.

When you have learned this, you bear your own grief, your own disappointment, more proudly, knowing that thus you quiet the gnawing upon your heart.
